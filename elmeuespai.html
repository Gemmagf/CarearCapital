Two years in a spreadsheet

Four columns kept by hand every day since 2024: the date, hours fasted, what exercise was done, and a line about the day. Weight when she felt like weighing. Period when it came. That spreadsheet is the insight — people who track seriously already keep the whole picture in one place, and every app on the market hands them a fifth of it.

Try the product

The build below is the real app, running in this page. Move through the tabs, start a session, watch the figures. Everything in it is invented data.

  1. One record, not five apps

    Fasting, weight, activity, cycle and journal are fields on the same day. Tapping any date opens all five at once. Today that is a workout app, a fasting timer, a period tracker, a weight log and a diary — four subscriptions and no single view.

  2. The tracking drives the training

    This is the part nobody ships. Period days swap jumps for low-impact cardio without dropping the intensity. A day already full of cycling gets a gentler suggestion. The record is an input, not a separate tab.

  3. It adapts from one tap

    Too easy, just right, too hard. That single rating moves a number that sets work time, rest, and which of the 238 exercises are allowed to appear at all. No onboarding quiz, no plan to choose.

  4. It costs nothing to run

    No video, no server, no analytics, no accounts. The record lives on the device. A user costs the same as no user.

Fasting, treated as a habit rather than a stopwatch

The app stores a start time, not a running timer, so closing it or turning the phone off changes nothing. Opening it mid-fast is the moment that matters: it does not just show a counter, it says what to do with it, and the wording changes as the hours pass.

ElapsedWhat it says
under 12 hGoing well. 3 h 20 min to your usual 16.
14–16 hVery close. If you can, stretch it a little.
16–18 hTarget met. Stop whenever you like, or push on.
over 20 hThat is a long time. Better to stop and eat properly.

The last row is a product position. An app that only ever says keep going to someone counting fasting hours is not neutral software, and building the brake in from day one is cheaper than retrofitting it after somebody gets hurt.

Two decisions worth defending

Hours land on the day the fast began. A fast starting at 8 pm and ending at noon belongs to the evening it started, which is how she had always written it. Matching the habit beat matching the clock.

Stopping takes two taps, not a dialog. The button asks Stop at 15 h 12 min? and waits three seconds. It also works inside a sandboxed page, where browser dialogs are blocked and would silently do nothing — which is exactly the bug the first version shipped with.

What it shows over time

Twenty-eight days of bars, pink once a day clears sixteen hours. Not a streak and not a score: whether the pattern held.

The cycle is an input, not another tab

Cycle tracking is a crowded category and a solved one. What is not solved is the connection: knowing where someone is in their cycle and doing nothing with it in the part of the app that asks them to work.

Here, period days keep the level and the length — she still wants to sweat — but high-impact movements are filtered out and replaced with low-impact cardio. The home screen says gentle, still sweating, not rest day. Marking the first day is the whole interaction.

Found in use: the first version let an unmarked period run until today, so days long past still softened the workouts. It now assumes the user's own average length. Small bug, and a good example of why one real user beats a hundred imagined ones.

The calendar paints marked days solid and predictions as a dashed outline, so a guess never looks like a fact. Cycles are not 28 days and get less regular around any change in contraception; recomputing from the user's own history beats shipping a default and calling it a prediction.

The journal is what makes the numbers readable

The original spreadsheet's fourth column was the interesting one. Next to 19 and Pilates there would be a line about a trip, an argument, a bad week.

A fortnight of short fasts and no exercise looks like a lapse in a chart, and looks like something else entirely once you read the line beside it. A tracker that keeps only numbers is a tracker that will misjudge its user — and the ones that do get deleted in January.

So the diary is a field on the day, not a feature. Importing the old spreadsheet brought two years of those lines across intact, filling empty fields only and never overwriting anything already written.

Writing in the past

People do not journal on schedule. Any day in the calendar opens a sheet with all five fields, stored under that date — the weight lands in the right place on the chart, the period day counts for the cycle maths.

Why that matters commercially

The journal is the switching cost. Exercise data is replaceable and everyone's looks the same. Two years of someone's own writing, attached to their own numbers, is not something they export to a competitor.

238 exercises, no camera, no licensing

Every competitor in this category either licenses an animation library or films a studio shoot. Both are a permanent cost line and a hard ceiling on catalogue size.

Here the figure is a rig: seven joints, and each pose is twelve angles plus a hip position. An exercise is two to four poses, interpolated. Adding a movement costs twelve numbers, not a shoot. Floor poses are solved with two-link inverse kinematics so hands and feet land on the mat.

The honest limitation: the rig is a side view, and movements that belong in a front view are drawn with the same skeleton. It shows.

DecisionConsequence

One tap after each session moves a single number between 1 and 5: +0.34 too easy, +0.10 just right, −0.28 too hard. Up is faster than down, so a bad week does not undo a good month.

Where it stands, honestly

Built and in daily use

The whole product above works today: the five-part record, the adaptive session engine, 238 exercises, offline use, cross-device sync, import and export. One person has used it every day since it shipped, and most of what is good in it came from her complaints.

Not built

No accounts, no multi-user backend, no payments, no App Store presence. The sync is last-write-wins and the lock screen is cosmetic — both fine for one person and wrong for a hundred.

What would need proving

Three questions, in order. Does the adaptive loop hold someone who is not the person it was built for? Does keeping five things in one record change retention against single-purpose apps? And will people pay for a product whose main promise is that their data never leaves the device?

None of that is answered yet, and a prototype with one user cannot answer it. What it does show is that the expensive parts — the catalogue, the drawing system, the adaptive engine — already exist and cost nothing to operate.

No framework, no build step

Three JavaScript files and a stylesheet, served as they are. It runs if nobody touches it for five years, and whoever inherits it can read it.

Private by construction, not by policy

A health record and a diary in one file is exactly what you do not hand to a third party. No accounts, no analytics, no server to breach — and the whole history exports to a file on request. That is a claim that survives due diligence, because there is nothing to audit.
